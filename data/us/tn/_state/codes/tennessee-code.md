For an existing statute, open **Tennessee Code** from the [General Assembly’s legislation page](https://wapp.capitol.tn.gov/apps/BillInfo/Legislation). Use the section number from the document you are reading. The legislature links to LexisNexis for the Code. If the text does not display in an automated reader, open the official link in a browser. Justia also has an unofficial copy you can browse by year. Check the edition and confirm the wording against the official text. [Library of Congress guide](https://guides.loc.gov/law-us-tennessee/legislative) · [Justia’s Tennessee Code](https://law.justia.com/codes/tennessee/)

If you have a bill number, first [look up its history](../services/track-a-bill.md). Once a general bill becomes law, the Secretary of State assigns it a public chapter number. A public chapter shows the change lawmakers enacted, including when it takes effect. The Code organizes statutes by subject and incorporates those changes. [How a bill becomes a law](https://wapp.capitol.tn.gov/apps/Information/About/BillToLaw) · [How to read a public chapter](../services/read-a-public-chapter.md)

## If the bill became a private act

A **private chapter** can change how a particular city or county operates. Look it up under **Private Acts Index** on the [State Library and Archives publications page](https://digitaltennessee.tnsos.gov/sos/). For a county, the University of Tennessee also lets you search by county, subject, or year and chapter number. Its historical notes flag acts that have gone out of effect. [County private acts search](https://www.ctas.tennessee.edu/private-acts)

Read the chapter’s local approval provision. It tells you whether to look for a vote of the local governing body or a referendum. For a county act, ask the county legislative body or the county election commission, whichever handled the vote, for the result it certified to the Secretary of State. [University of Tennessee guide to local approval](https://www.ctas.tennessee.edu/eli/private-acts)
