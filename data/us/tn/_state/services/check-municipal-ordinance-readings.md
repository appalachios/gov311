If an agenda lists a “first reading,” check the rule for that town before counting on another meeting to speak. Start with the current charter. Look for the section on passing ordinances and any exceptions for the kind of proposal you’re following. A rule can specify how many times the board must consider a proposal, how much time must pass between votes and when the ordinance takes effect. [Find the town’s charter](https://www.mtas.tennessee.edu/charters-codes/charters)

If the charter gives no number, look in the municipal code under “General Administration,” “Board,” or “Rules of Procedure.” Check the date of the code and ask the recorder for amendments and the board’s current standing rules. Tennessee’s Municipal Technical Advisory Service explains that one reading can suffice when the charter is silent. A local rule or a law covering the subject can add steps. A rezoning, for example, can require a separate public hearing. [Find the local code](https://www.mtas.tennessee.edu/charters-codes/codes) · [Tennessee ordinance procedures](https://www.mtas.tennessee.edu/reference/procedures-adopting-ordinances) · [Municipal zoning hearings](follow-a-municipal-zoning-amendment.md)

Minutes can show how many times the board voted on one ordinance. To establish what future boards must do, find the provision that requires those votes. In *State ex rel. Balsinger v. Town of Madisonville*, the Tennessee Supreme Court rejected the argument that the town had to use three separate meetings simply because earlier boards had done so. The town’s charter prescribed no such requirement. [Tennessee Supreme Court opinion, 435 S.W.2d 803, 805 (1968)](https://law.justia.com/cases/tennessee/supreme-court/1968/435-s-w-2d-803-2.html)
